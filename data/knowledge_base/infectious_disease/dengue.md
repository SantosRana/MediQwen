# Dengue (Break-bone Fever)

## Overview
- Viral infection spread by mosquitoes, especially *Aedes aegypti*.
- More common in tropical and subtropical climates.
- Most cases mild or asymptomatic; recovery in 1–2 weeks.
- Severe dengue can be fatal and requires hospitalization.
- No specific treatment; managed with pain relief and supportive care.
- Prevention relies on avoiding mosquito bites and vector control.

## Symptoms
- **Onset:** 4–10 days after infection, lasting 2–7 days.
- **Common:** high fever (40°C/104°F), severe headache, pain behind eyes, muscle/joint pain, nausea, vomiting, swollen glands, rash.
- **Severe dengue (after fever subsides):** abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, rapid breathing, bleeding gums/nose, fatigue, restlessness, blood in vomit/stool, thirst, pale/cold skin, weakness.
- Severe cases can lead to death.
- Post-recovery fatigue may last weeks.

## Diagnostics
- Laboratory and point-of-care tests essential but uneven globally.
- Methods: nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs), ELISAs, rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs).
- Direct detection: virus isolation, molecular detection, antigen testing.
- Quality and performance vary across systems.

## Treatment
- No specific antiviral treatment.
- Pain managed with paracetamol (acetaminophen).
- Avoid NSAIDs (ibuprofen, aspirin) due to bleeding risk.
- Severe cases: hospitalization, fluid management, supportive care.

## Global Burden
- Cases rose from 505,430 (2000) to 14.6 million (2024).
- Over 12,000 deaths reported in 2024.
- Endemic in >100 countries; spreading to new regions (Europe, Eastern Mediterranean).
- Americas contributed >13 million cases in 2024.
- Estimated 390 million infections annually; 96 million symptomatic.
- 5.6 billion people at risk globally.

## Transmission
- **Mosquito bite:** infected female *Aedes aegypti* (primary vector).
- Extrinsic incubation period: 8–12 days at 25–28°C.
- **Human-to-mosquito:** occurs in symptomatic, pre-symptomatic, and asymptomatic individuals.
- Viremia lasts 4–5 days, up to 12 days.
- **Maternal transmission:** rare, linked to timing of infection in pregnancy; may cause pre-term birth or low birthweight.
- **Other rare modes:** blood transfusion, organ donation, transovarial transmission in mosquitoes.

## Risk Factors
- Secondary infection increases risk of severe dengue.
- Rapid urbanization, poor water storage, high population density.
- Climate change (temperature, rainfall, humidity).
- Fragile health systems, weak surveillance, political instability.
- Community behaviors (water storage, plant-keeping, protective practices).

## Prevention & Control
- Mosquitoes active during the day.
- **Personal protection:** long clothing, mosquito nets, window screens, repellents (DEET, Picaridin, IR3535), coils/vaporizers.
- **Vector control:** environmental management, waste disposal, cleaning water containers weekly, insecticides in outdoor storage.
- **Self-care if infected:** rest, fluids, paracetamol, avoid NSAIDs, monitor for severe symptoms.
- **Vaccination:** QDenga licensed in some countries, recommended for ages 6–16 in high transmission settings; other vaccines under evaluation.

## Notes
- Dengue is spreading to new regions due to climate change and vector adaptation.
- Surveillance, diagnostics, and community engagement are critical to control.
- Severe dengue requires urgent medical care; case fatality can be reduced with timely treatment.
