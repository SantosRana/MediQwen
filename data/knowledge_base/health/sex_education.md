# Comprehensive Sexuality Education (CSE)

## Key Facts
- Provides **age-appropriate, scientifically accurate, culturally relevant** information on bodies, health, relationships, and rights.  
- Promotes self-awareness, respect, and equitable norms.  
- Evidence shows CSE **delays sexual initiation**, reduces risk-taking, and increases contraceptive use.  
- CSE does **not** increase sexual activity or encourage earlier behaviour.  
- Programmes that combine delaying sex + contraceptive use are more effective than abstinence-only.  
- Access remains uneven; quality varies globally.  

## Overview
- Curriculum-based, incremental approach covering cognitive, emotional, physical, and social aspects of sexuality.  
- Delivered in formal (schools) and non-formal settings.  
- Helps children and youth understand their bodies, build healthy relationships, make informed decisions, and protect health.  

## What CSE Covers
- **Relationships:** families, friendships, love, respect, communication, parenting.  
- **Human rights & values:** rights, responsibilities, diversity, inclusion.  
- **Gender equality:** stereotypes, discrimination, power dynamics, gender-based violence.  
- **Consent & safety:** privacy, bodily integrity, reporting abuse, safe digital use.  
- **Life skills:** decision-making, negotiation, media literacy, help-seeking.  
- **Human development:** anatomy, puberty, menstruation, reproduction, fertility, body image.  
- **Sexuality:** identity, behaviour, sexual development.  
- **Sexual & reproductive health:** contraception, pregnancy prevention, HIV/STIs, stigma reduction, adolescent-friendly services.  

## Guiding Framework
- Rights-based, learner-centred, adaptable to local contexts.  
- Emphasizes inclusion, empathy, responsibility, gender equality.  
- Requires collaboration with educators, health experts, parents, and youth.  

## Why CSE Matters
- Without CSE, young people may be unprepared for puberty, vulnerable to abuse, or misinformed.  
- CSE equips them to make informed choices, communicate effectively, and protect health.  

## Evidence
- High-quality CSE:  
  - Increases knowledge, positive attitudes, and protective behaviours.  
  - Delays sexual initiation, increases condom/contraceptive use, reduces partners and risk-taking.  
  - Associated with lower unintended pregnancy and reduced STI risk.  
- No evidence that CSE increases sexual activity.  

## When & Where
- UN guidance: start at age 5 (formal education).  
- Lifelong process, reinforced at home and in communities.  
- Incremental learning: early childhood → puberty → adolescence.  
- Consistent messaging across home, school, community strengthens effectiveness.  

## Global Situation
- Progress made, but access uneven due to social norms, misconceptions, teacher capacity gaps.  
- Vulnerable groups (out-of-school youth, humanitarian settings, girls, LGBTQ+, disabled, rural adolescents) often excluded.  
- Scaling up requires inclusive materials, participatory methods, trained facilitators, and linkage to adolescent-friendly health services.  

## WHO Response
- Supports countries by:  
  - Researching and testing improved content/delivery.  
  - Synthesizing evidence and programmatic experiences.  
  - Providing technical support for adaptation, implementation, monitoring, evaluation.  
  - Promoting gender equality, safeguarding, safe learning environments.  
  - Strengthening links between CSE and adolescent health services.  
  - Advocacy to highlight CSE’s role in health and well-being.  

## Notes
- CSE is a cornerstone of adolescent health and rights.  
- Effective programmes require political will, trained educators, and community support.
