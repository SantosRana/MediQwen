# Paralysis

## Overview
- Paralysis is the inability to move some or all of the body.
- Can be temporary or permanent depending on the cause.
- May start suddenly, gradually, or come and go.

## Symptoms
You may have paralysis if:
- Cannot move part or all of face/body
- Weakness or floppiness in muscles
- Numbness, pain, or tingling
- Muscle stiffness, spasms, or twitches

## Emergency (999 / A&E)
Call emergency services immediately if paralysis or weakness:
- Happens suddenly
- Occurs after head, neck, or back injury
- Causes problems with speech, breathing, or swallowing
- Affects one side of the face (drooping) or one arm (cannot lift both arms)
- Involves loss of sensation or tingling in face/body

**Note:** Do not drive yourself to A&E. Ambulance transport is required.

## Causes
Paralysis can result from many conditions affecting muscles and nerves. Common causes:
- Transient ischaemic attack (TIA) or stroke (face droop, arm weakness)
- Head injury
- Spinal cord injury
- Bell’s palsy (facial paralysis)
- Multiple sclerosis
- Sleep paralysis (temporary)
- Muscular dystrophy (long-term)
- Brain tumour
- Certain cancers (e.g., head or neck cancer)

## When to See a GP
Seek medical advice if paralysis or weakness:
- Starts gradually
- Is slowly worsening
- Comes and goes

## Treatments
Treatment depends on the cause.  
- Temporary paralysis may resolve without treatment.  
- Supportive therapies include:
  - Physiotherapy (maintain strength and muscle mass)
  - Occupational therapy (adapt to daily tasks)
  - Medicines (relieve pain, stiffness, spasms)

## Notes
- Paralysis can be a sign of serious neurological or systemic illness.
- Early medical evaluation is critical for sudden or severe cases.
- Rehabilitation and therapy improve quality of life in long-term paralysis.
